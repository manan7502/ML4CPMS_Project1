1. The Four Features Added in Stage 1

1.1 Shannon Entropy of the Document Token Distribution (\(H\))

For document \(d\) with token vocabulary \(V_d\) and empirical probabilities \(p(t) = \frac{\text{count}(t)}{L_d}\):

$$H(d) = -\sum_{t \in V_d} p(t) \log_2 p(t)$$

Why it is added: In modern autoregressive language generation, sampling decoders employ nucleus sampling (top-\(p\)) and temperature scaling (\(T < 1.0\)). These decoding algorithms truncate unlikely tail tokens and compress probability mass into high-confidence vocabulary. Consequently, AI-generated essays exhibit a distinct token distribution entropy profile compared to human essays.

1.2 Guiraud's Index / Root Type-Token Ratio (\(\text{Root\_TTR}\))

Classical Type-Token Ratio (\(\text{TTR} = \frac{|V_d|}{L_d}\)) suffers from an inherent mathematical flaw: as an essay lengthens, the author inevitably repeats ubiquitous grammatical function words ("the", "of", "and"), artificially deflating TTR for longer texts. Guiraud's Index corrects this by normalizing vocabulary size by the square root of document length:

$$\text{Root\_TTR} = \frac{|V_d|}{\sqrt{L_d}}$$

Why it is added: Because human essays in our dataset are on average \(1.78\times\) longer than machine essays, classical TTR is heavily confounded. Guiraud's index is length-invariant, capturing the true underlying vocabulary breadth.

1.3 Hapax Legomena Ratio (\(\text{Hapax\_Ratio}\))

The proportion of vocabulary words that appear exactly once in the entire essay:

$$\text{Hapax\_Ratio} = \frac{|\{t \in V_d : \text{count}(t) = 1\}|}{L_d}$$

Why it is added: Human writers frequently incorporate idiosyncratic context words, specialized domain terms, or narrative analogies that appear only once. LLMs, constrained by attention priors, tend to reuse consistent terminology throughout an essay.

1.4 Maximum Term Domination Ratio (\(\text{Max\_Rep}\))

$$\text{Max\_Rep} = \frac{\max_{t \in V_d} \text{count}(t)}{L_d}$$

Why it is added: Measures whether an essay's token distribution is dominated by a single hyper-frequent topic keyword or pronoun, indicating potential repetitive prompt fixation.
