1. Theoretical Foundation of Log Document Frequency Ratios

Generative language models possess systemic corpus-level vocabulary biases: certain token families are disproportionately over-represented, while human colloquialisms and idiosyncratic phrasings are under-represented.

For each token \(t\) in the 12,582-token unigram vocabulary, its empirical log-odds likelihood ratio between Machine (Class B) and Human (Class A) documents is defined under Laplace smoothing as:

$$r_t = \ln \left(\frac{\text{DF}_B(t) + 1}{|D_B| + 2}\right) - \ln \left(\frac{\text{DF}_A(t) + 1}{|D_A| + 2}\right)$$

where \(|D_B| = 6,837\) and \(|D_A| = 3,699\).

  • When \(r_t > 0\), token \(t\) has a higher empirical probability of appearing in a machine-generated text.
  • When \(r_t < 0\), token \(t\) is strongly indicative of human authorship.

2. The Failed Experiment: Why Pointwise NBSVM Failed

In classical text classification literature (Wang & Manning, ACL 2012), Naive Bayes SVM (NBSVM) achieves strong results by multiplying the sparse feature vector element-wise by the log-count ratio vector:

$$\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{r} \odot \mathbf{x}$$
NBSVM Empirical Result: Accuracy Dropped to 89.18%
When we implemented pointwise NBSVM weighting across our cumulative 1-5 gram feature space (\(P = 248,349\)), accuracy plummeted from 90.31% to 89.18% (-1.13%).

Why did pointwise NBSVM fail on high-order N-grams?

  1. Laplace Distortion on Rare N-Grams: In higher-order \(N\)-grams (4-grams and 5-grams), tens of thousands of features appear only 1 or 2 times across the entire dataset. For these rare features, the Laplace pseudo-count \(+1\) dominates the true frequency, injecting massive estimation noise into the coordinates.
  2. Margin Distortion: Pointwise multiplication magnifies the magnitude of rare, noisy features, severely degrading the margin geometry of the Linear SVM hyperplane.

3. The Resolution: Document-Level Moment Pooling

Instead of modifying individual sparse coordinates, we mapped the token sequence of each essay \(d = (t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n)\) to its corresponding unigram log-odds sequence \(\{r_{t_i}\}\) and extracted five continuous summary moments:

$$\mu_{\text{aff}} = \frac{1}{|d|} \sum_{t \in d} r_t, \quad \sigma_{\text{aff}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{|d|} \sum_{t \in d} (r_t - \mu_{\text{aff}})^2}$$ $$\max_{\text{aff}} = \max_{t \in d} r_t, \quad \min_{\text{aff}} = \min_{t \in d} r_t, \quad \text{Pos\_Ratio} = \frac{|\{t \in d : r_t > 0\}|}{|d|}$$

Why Pooling Works: Document-level pooling smooths out token-level variance via the Central Limit Theorem. The resulting summary statistics represent the aggregate machine affinity distribution of the entire essay as a compact, continuous descriptor.
